A product thought experiment

Banana Cards

Trading cards the Banana Ball way: value from being there, not from scarcity.

Concept to working demo · Not affiliated with the Savannah Bananas, Fans First Entertainment or the Banana Ball Championship League

Slide 1 notes. Open with the one-line idea: Banana Ball rewrote baseball around fans; Banana Cards does the same for trading cards. This is a thought experiment, built end to end: the opportunity, the value model, the decisions and tradeoffs, and a working demo. Say up front that it isn’t affiliated with the team or league.

The opportunity

Cards are where Banana Ball hasn’t arrived yet

What Banana Ball changed

  • A two-hour game
  • Fans catch foul balls for outs
  • Players dance; the crowd is part of the show

What its cards still are

  • Ordinary baseball cards, new stats
  • Value from scarcity and chase cards
  • Resale as the reason to care

Bring the Banana Ball ethos to trading cards. It’s a gap the brand’s philosophy hasn’t reached, more than a broken problem.

Banana Cards · 2

Slide 2 notes. Banana Ball turned baseball’s conventions upside down to put fans first. Trading cards are one of the few places it hasn’t done that: today’s cards run on the same value logic as any baseball card. Be honest that this isn’t fixing something broken; it’s extending a philosophy to a place it hasn’t reached.

The core inversion

Traditional cards

Value = scarcity + resale + “will it appreciate?”

Banana Cards

Value = what you experienced + who you shared it with

Scarcity can exist. It’s never the measure, and the product never creates or rewards it.

Banana Cards · 3

Slide 3 notes. This is the whole thesis on one slide. Traditional value is scarcity, resale and speculation. Banana Cards inverts it: value is the experience of getting the card and the people you shared it with. Note the principle: we don’t try to police a resale market, because anything tradeable can be priced somewhere. We make sure the product never manufactures scarcity. If cards start selling for real money, that’s a signal the design let scarcity creep in.

Five sources of value

What makes a card worth having

1

Presence

You had to be there. Lives in keepsakes.

2

Story

Characters and bits, more than stats: the tux, the dancing umpire.

3

Trading

The social ritual of the swap, in person, never a price.

4

Completion

Themed sets: the payoff is finishing the story.

5

Access

Small, real perks: a shoutout, a clip, a photo op.

None of the five depends on a card being rare. All five are built into the demo.

Banana Cards · 4

Slide 4 notes. These five replace scarcity as the reason a card matters. Presence is first and is the one the design protects most carefully; it lives in keepsakes, which we’ll see in a minute. Sets and perks are the answer to the between-tours problem: most fans see a team about once a year, and these give the collection a reason to be opened in between.

How it works

A cheap code out there, a rich card on your phone

1 · Get a code

In the program, on a seat, from a player, on the stream, in a post

2 · Scan it

Any phone camera. No app; a new fan joins with just a name

3 · Tap to reveal

The card waits face down. Flip it to see what you got

4 · Collect

Set progress right away; finished sets unlock perks

One gesture for everything. Redeeming, trading and passing a card on all start with a scan.

Breakeven by design. The physical side is a cheap, single-use code; the rich card costs nothing to copy.

Banana Cards · 5

Slide 5 notes. Getting the card is the core experience: every fan does it first, and plenty will never trade. So it’s fast: under a minute, before first pitch or between innings, then the phone goes back in the pocket. The app never needs attention during a bit, and codes stay claimable after the game. The code was originally imagined as a token handed out at the gate, but it can live anywhere: a program, a seat, a player’s hat, a stream overlay.

Two kinds of cards

How you earned it decides whether you can trade it

Pool cards

From a general code: a random card from a visible pool, every card equally likely.

Tradeable. Value from sets, story and the swap.

Keepsakes

From a specific code: one card, because of something you did.

Never traded. Presence stays with the fan who was there.

Pools start around 24 cards per team per season: too big to finish alone, easy to finish by trading with your section. No tiers, no “rare” labels.

Banana Cards · 6

Slide 6 notes. This came out of auditing my own assumptions. If a “you were there” card can be traded, presence ends up with people who weren’t there. And a reveal needs surprise, but surprise usually brings rarity back. The answer: two kinds of cards. Pool cards come from general codes, drawn when you scan from a visible pool with equal odds, and they trade freely. Keepsakes come from a specific code, like the one in a player’s hat when you stay for an autograph, and they never move. A keepsake can be scarce, but because it never trades, scarcity never becomes its value: scarce like a signed ball, not like a chase card. Rough math: a 24-card pool takes about 90 pulls to finish alone; a section of 50 fans turns up most of it.

Trading

Social, not financial, by design

In person only

Both fans scan and both confirm. It works anywhere two fans meet, not only at the ballpark.

No marketplace grammar

No prices, no rarity counts, no browsable list of strangers’ cards.

Sets give the reason

“Finishes your set” on a friend’s card tells you what to ask for.

A digital trade screen drifts toward looking like a shop. That’s the product’s biggest risk, so every trade screen is designed against it.

Banana Cards · 7

Slide 7 notes. Choosing a single-use code over a physical card moved all the weight of “social, not financial” onto the trade screen. Two hard constraints follow. First, trades require two fans to be physically together: a mutual QR scan, the same gesture as redeeming. Together, not necessarily at the ballpark, which matters for fans without tickets. Second, no marketplace grammar: if the UI reads like a shop, the premise dies in the UI whatever is true underneath. Sets supply a reason to trade that’s about the collection, never about value.

Banana Cards · 8

Slide 8 notes. Banana Ball tickets are very hard to get. If the best cards required attendance, the product would reward the lucky few, the opposite of Fans First. Streamed games are a big part of how the fan base grows, so streams carry both kinds of card. Stream cards are live-only: “you were watching live” is the online version of “you had to be there.” Social drops have a plain end date and no countdowns, no “only 100 left,” no follow-to-win. Every card is honest about where it came from, and that label stays with it after a trade.

Fan base growth

Every fan arrives with a reason to bring another

Pass It On

Your first card comes with one to give away. Only a brand-new fan can receive it.

No-install first trade

A trade QR opens in any phone camera. A newcomer types a name and trades on the spot.

Players and teams as channels. They post their own drops and grow their own following, along with Banana Ball’s.

Banana Cards · 9

Slide 9 notes. Banana Cards doesn’t need to make money; specialty items are priced to break even. Its return is fan base growth. Two loops are built: Pass It On solves cold start, since every new fan arrives holding a reason to recruit another; and the no-install first trade means the person next to you can join in seconds. The business case for social drops rests on an assumption I flagged: that player and team pay includes bonuses tied to audience growth. It still needs a source.

Decisions and tradeoffs

Four calls, and what each one cost

DecisionThe obvious choiceWhat I choseWhat it cost
What the fan holdsA printed cardA single-use code; the card is digitalA physical keepsake; risks reading as an NFT
Where presence livesOn every cardKeepsakes never trade; pool cards trade freelyA traded card no longer proves you were there
KidsKid profiles, parental consent13+ accounts; families share an adult accountA kid’s own “my collection”
Resale marketsTry to stop themNever create scarcity; real prices are a diagnosticNo control over what happens off-platform

Banana Cards · 11

Slide 11 notes. Each row is a place where the obvious answer would have undercut the thesis. A printed card fails breakeven across six teams and leaves remote fans out, so the physical touch became a cheap code, and positioning has to address the NFT association head-on. Presence moved into keepsakes so trading can’t dilute it. The kids decision came from noticing that quick join, which asks only for a name, conflicted with COPPA; rather than build parental consent now, accounts are 13+ with a neutral year-of-birth question, and families share a grown-up’s account, which also means a parent is part of every kid’s trade. Finally, I stopped trying to prevent a market: anything tradeable can be priced somewhere, so the goal is that the product never manufactures scarcity. Not legal advice; the age screen needs a lawyer’s review before real fans.

Assumptions

I audited my own assumptions

5 stated up front, 14 surfaced on review, each marked accepted, mitigated or open.

Resolved by design

Presence survives trading? Now it lives in keepsakes.

Suspense without rarity? Equal odds from a visible pool.

Quick joiners are adults? Accounts are 13+.

The UI stops a market? Accepted: we don’t police it.

Still open

Remote fans have someone to trade with.

A digital card feels like a keepsake, and outlasts its service.

More fans is good while tickets are scarce.

Player pay tracks audience growth. Needs a source.

Banana Cards · 12

Slide 12 notes. As a thought experiment, this is built on assumptions rather than research, so I made them explicit. The left column shows four that changed the design: the pool and keepsake split, equal-odds pools, the 13+ decision, and dropping the idea of policing a market. The right column is what I’d test or source next. A ticketless fan with no local fans can collect but never trade. A physical card outlives its maker; a digital one doesn’t. Growth without seats could grow frustration unless the payoff is streams, merch or new markets. And the social-drop business case rests on player pay being tied to audience growth, which I haven’t sourced.

Guardrails

Hard constraints on every decision

No marketplace grammar: no prices, rarity counts or browsable inventories

No manipulative design: no streaks, daily-login rewards or fake urgency

No paid randomness: if anything ever costs money, you see exactly what’s in it

Nobody misses the show: no notifications during a game; claim cards later

Minimum fan data: never sold or shared; accounts are 13+

Banana Cards · 13

Slide 13 notes. These aren’t nice-to-haves; they sit alongside the value model as constraints any future feature has to pass. They’re why sets never expire, why there are no countdown timers on drops, and why the phone rule exists: the app never needs attention during a bit.

How we’d know it’s working

Better nights, and more fans

Fan base growth

  • New fans per Pass It On card
  • Stream and drop claims that become collectors
  • First-timers who arrive holding cards

Fan experience

  • Share of fans at a game who claim a card
  • Collectors who trade at least once
  • Collectors who return between tours

Signals of harm

  • App use during the show (should be near zero)
  • Real resale prices: scarcity crept in
  • Complaints from families or first-timers

Caution: while an account costs only a name, one person can inflate growth counts. The metrics need that guard before they mean much.

Banana Cards · 14

Slide 14 notes. Banana Ball’s north star is fan experience, so Banana Cards is judged on two things together: does it make the night better, and does it grow the fan base. Exact metrics are still to be picked; these are candidates. The harm signals matter as much as the growth ones. Real resale prices aren’t something to police; they tell us where the design let scarcity in.

What’s next

Banana Cards · 15

Slide 15 notes. Close on what’s next. In the demo: general codes should draw from pools at scan time, the drop-based sets get redefined as pool series, and the age question gets built. Card drops as bits are the cheapest path into making cards part of the show, since they reuse the drop mechanism. Outside the code: the core thesis, that fans value the story of getting a card over its rarity, is accepted, not validated. Fan conversations are the first real test, along with sourcing the player-pay claim. Then open it up for questions.
